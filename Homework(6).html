Exercise 2: Statistical Independence

1. Theoretical Concepts of Independence

In probability and statistics, two random variables X and F are statistically independent when knowing the outcome of one provides no information about the outcome of the other. Formally, their joint probability equals the product of their marginal probabilities for all category pairs (i, j):

P(X = x_i, F = f_j) = P(X = x_i) * P(F = f_j) <=> P(F = f_j | X = x_i) = P(F = f_j)

When conditional distributions differ across categories of X, the variables are statistically dependent. To test independence on categorical or grouped bivariate data under the null hypothesis H0 (independence), we compare observed counts O_ij against expected counts E_ij computed from the row total R_i, column total C_j, and total sample size N with degrees of freedom df = (r - 1)(c - 1):

E_ij = (R_i * C_j) / N | chi^2 = Sum [ (O_ij - E_ij)^2 / E_ij ] | df = (r - 1)(c - 1)

6. Conclusions: Statistical Association vs. Causality

A significant Chi-square test indicates statistical association (dependence) in the observed sample, but statistical association alone does not prove a direct cause-and-effect relationship between X and F:

Dimension Statistical Association / Dependence Causality
Meaning The joint or conditional distributions of X and F depart from independence (H0 rejected). Changing or intervening on X directly causes a change in F.
Symmetry Symmetric: association between X and F is identical regardless of which variable is listed first. Directional (asymmetric): X causing F does not imply that F causes X.
Confounders & Study Design Can arise from confounding variables (e.g., prior background, motivation), sampling bias, or chance. Requires controlled experiments, temporal precedence, or causal modeling beyond a contingency table.
